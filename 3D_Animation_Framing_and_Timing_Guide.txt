MODULE 1: UNDERSTANDING FPS (FRAMES PER SECOND) & TIMELINE MATH
================================================================================

1. WHAT IS A FRAME?
   A "Frame" is a single still image. Animation is simply a series of still 
   images played in rapid succession to create the illusion of motion, just 
   like a physical paper flipbook.

2. WHAT IS FPS?
   FPS stands for "Frames Per Second". It tells you how many still images 
   Blender will play in ONE real-world second.

   * 24 FPS (Cinema / Hollywood Standard):
     - Plays 24 frames every second.
     - Used by Disney, Pixar, Hollywood feature films, and Anime.
     - Why animators love 24 FPS: Easy division!
       * 1.0 Second  = 24 Frames
       * 0.5 Second  = 12 Frames (Half)
       * 0.25 Second = 6 Frames (Quarter)
       * 2.0 Seconds = 48 Frames

   * 30 FPS (Television / YouTube / Social Media Standard):
     - Plays 30 frames every second.
     - Used for YouTube videos, Instagram Reels, TikTok, and TV broadcasts.
     - Timing Breakdown:
       * 1.0 Second  = 30 Frames
       * 0.5 Second  = 15 Frames (Half)
       * 0.25 Second = 7-8 Frames (Quarter)
       * 2.0 Seconds = 60 Frames

   * 60 FPS (Video Games & Live Motion Capture):
     - Plays 60 frames every second.
     - Used for high-end PC/console games and live webcam tracking.
     - Extremely crisp and smooth, but requires twice as many frames to render.

3. THE GOLDEN TIMING CONVERSION FORMULA:
   --------------------------------------------------------
   Target Frames = Real-Life Time in Seconds x Project FPS
   --------------------------------------------------------
   Example: If a character takes a 1.5 second drink of water at 30 FPS:
            1.5 x 30 = 45 Frames!

MODULE 2: THE CORE RULE OF ANIMATION SPEED (TIMING VS SPACING)
================================================================================

Why do manual animations sometimes look "too fast" or "too slow"?

1. THE SPEED EQUATION:
   Speed = Distance Moved / Number of Frames Between Keyframes

   * FEW FRAMES (4 to 6 frames apart)  = RAPID / EXPLOSIVE SPEED (Snap)
   * MANY FRAMES (20 to 30 frames apart) = SLOW / HEAVY SPEED (Weight)

2. COMMON STUDENT MISTAKE:
   Placing keyframes at random intervals (e.g. Frame 1, Frame 7, Frame 13, 
   Frame 29) without planning the real-world rhythm. 
   Result: The character suddenly speeds up, then lags, creating a jerky look.

3. THE FIX (THE BEAT ALIGNMENT RULE):
   In the Dope Sheet, always align keyframes across different bones (hips, 
   feet, arms, head) on the same rhythmic "beats" (e.g. Columns 1, 15, 30, 45).

MODULE 4: MULTI-ACTION SEQUENCING (CHAINING WALK -> PUNCH -> JUMP)
================================================================================

How to build a complete 3-action sequence in the same continuous timeline:

SCENARIO: A character walks for 1 second, throws a punch for 0.5 seconds, 
          and then jumps over an obstacle for 1.5 seconds at 30 FPS.

TIMELINE BLUEPRINT (Total Duration = 90 Frames = 3.0 Seconds):

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SEGMENT 1: WALKING (1.0 Second = 30 Frames)
- Frames 1 to 30: Character completes a 2-step walk forward.
- Frame 30: Contact foot plants on ground, establishing solid combat base.

SEGMENT 2: PUNCH (0.5 Second = 15 Frames)
- Frame 31: Transition pose - Torso rotates and right fist cocks back (Anticipation).
- Frame 38: Maximum punch extension forward (-Y direction).
- Frame 45: Fist recoils back to defensive guard.

SEGMENT 3: JUMP & LAND (1.5 Seconds = 45 Frames)
- Frames 46 to 52: Deep crouch load (compressing hips & knees).
- Frames 53 to 65: Explosive takeoff & apex flight (sailing in an arc over the rod).
- Frames 66 to 78: Impact touchdown (absorbing landing shock).
- Frames 79 to 90: Rising smoothly back into a balanced standing ready pose!
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RULES FOR MULTI-ACTION SEQUENCING:
1. Never overlap start frames. (Action B start frame = Action A end frame + 1).
2. Always include 2 to 4 "Anticipation/Wind-up" frames before any big jump or hit.
3. Always include 4 to 8 "Recovery/Absorption" frames after any jump or landing.

MODULE 5: CAMERA FRAMING & CINEMATIC SHOT TYPES
================================================================================

Framing controls how the audience experiences your character's emotion & action:

1. SHOT SIZES (DISTANCE & HEIGHT):
   * CLOSE-UP (Distance: ~0.8m, Height: Head Level):
     - Frames the character's face, eyes, and expressions.
     - Best for: Dialogue, lip sync, emotional reactions.
   
   * MEDIUM SHOT (Distance: ~2.4m, Height: Chest Level):
     - Frames from the waist up to the head.
     - Best for: Hand gestures, holding props, combat punches & blocks.
   
   * WIDE SHOT (Distance: ~5.2m, Height: Full Body):
     - Shows full character from head to toe and the surrounding environment.
     - Best for: Parkour jumps, walk cycles on a path, whole fight scenes.

2. CAMERA ANGLES:
   * FRONT: Camera looks directly at the character's face.
   * THREE-QUARTER (45 Degrees): The most cinematic angle in filmmaking; reveals 
     depth, volume, and silhouette.
   * PROFILE (Side 90 Degrees): Perfect for checking foot arches and stride length.
   * LOW ANGLE (Worm's Eye): Makes the character look powerful, heroic, and tall.
   * HIGH ANGLE (Bird's Eye): Makes the character look vulnerable or showcases floor terrain.

3. OVER-THE-SHOULDER (OTS) DIALOGUE FRAMING:
   - Camera sits behind Actor A's shoulder looking directly at Actor B's face.
   - Used in every Hollywood dialogue and conversation scene.

MODULE 6: HOW TO FIX JITTER & SMOOTH OUT KEYFRAMES IN BLENDER
================================================================================

1. WHAT CAUSES JITTER?
   In Blender's Graph Editor, keyframes use Bezier curves. If tangent handles 
   overshoot above or below the target value, the bone "bounces" or stutters.

2. THE THREE WAYS TO FIX IT:
   * Method 1 (1-Click in HumanRigGenerator):
     - Go to the sidebar panel -> Procedural Animations.
     - Click "Smooth & Fix Jitter".
     - Automatically resets all curve handles to AUTO_CLAMPED, eliminating 
       speed spikes instantly!

   * Method 2 (Graph Editor Auto-Clamp):
     - Switch window to "Graph Editor".
     - Press 'A' to select all keyframe curves.
     - Press 'V' on your keyboard and choose "Automatic Clamped".

   * Method 3 (Dope Sheet Uniform Scaling):
     - If the animation is moving too fast or too slow overall:
     - Place timeline playhead at Frame 1.
     - Select all keys in the Dope Sheet ('A').
     - Press 'S' (Scale) and drag your mouse to stretch or tighten timing evenly!

MODULE 7: HANDS-ON STUDENT EXERCISES & PRACTICE ASSIGNMENTS
================================================================================

EXERCISE 1: THE TIMING TEST (30 FPS)
Goal: Animate a simple arm waving hand.
- Frame 1: Hand rests at hip.
- Frame 15 (0.5 sec): Hand raises high above shoulder.
- Frame 22: Wave left.
- Frame 29: Wave right.
- Frame 36: Wave left.
- Frame 45 (1.5 sec): Return hand down to hip.
Question for Student: How many total seconds did the wave take? (Answer: 1.5s)

EXERCISE 2: THE 3-POINT DIALOGUE & VAULT
Goal: Create a continuous 120-frame story scene at 30 FPS.
- Frames 1 to 30: Character walks along a curve path.
- Frames 31 to 60: Character stops, turns head, and points with hand.
- Frames 61 to 108: Character performs the one-hand rail vault jump.
- Frames 109 to 120: Character lands and stands in combat ready stance.
